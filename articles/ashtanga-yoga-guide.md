Ashtanga yoga has a reputation. It is described as athletic, disciplined and demanding, and its devotees often practice six days a week in the early morning. Yet underneath the reputation is a very structured and logical method, and many beginners can learn it with patience, modifications and good guidance. If you like routine, enjoy a challenge and want a practice that builds strength, stamina and focus, Ashtanga may suit you.

This guide explains what Ashtanga yoga is, how its practice is organized, what is in the primary series, and how a beginner can approach it safely. It also covers common mistakes and who should take care before starting.

## What is Ashtanga yoga?

"Ashtanga" is Sanskrit for "eight limbs." It refers to the eight-part path of yoga described in the ancient text known as the Yoga Sutras, attributed to Patanjali. The eight limbs are:

1. **Yama:** ethical restraints, such as non-violence and truthfulness
2. **Niyama:** personal observances, such as cleanliness and contentment
3. **Asana:** physical postures
4. **Pranayama:** breath practices
5. **Pratyahara:** turning the senses inward
6. **Dharana:** concentration
7. **Dhyana:** meditation
8. **Samadhi:** a state of deep absorption

Modern Ashtanga yoga, sometimes called Ashtanga vinyasa yoga, refers specifically to a system of postures taught by K. Pattabhi Jois in Mysore, India, in the twentieth century, and spread worldwide through his students. It emphasizes a fixed sequence of poses linked by breath and movement. Although it focuses on the third limb (asana), practitioners are encouraged to explore the others over time.

## How Ashtanga differs from other styles

- **Fixed sequence.** Unlike vinyasa, where each class differs, Ashtanga uses set series of poses practiced in the same order
- **Breath and gaze.** Practitioners use ujjayi breath (a smooth, audible breath) and fixed gaze points (drishti) to maintain focus
- **Vinyasa between poses.** Between many poses, you move through a sequence of plank, low push-up, upward-facing dog and downward-facing dog
- **Mysore style.** In traditional classes, students practice the sequence at their own pace while the teacher gives individual guidance, rather than following a led class
- **Progressive learning.** A teacher traditionally adds new poses only when you are ready, and often you do not move to the next pose until you have developed the previous one

MedlinePlus describes Ashtanga or power yoga as a more demanding workout in which you quickly move from one posture to another. It is on the more intense side of the yoga spectrum.

## The structure of a practice

A typical Ashtanga practice has three parts:

1. **Opening.** A short chant (optional) and Sun Salutations A and B to warm the body
2. **Standing poses.** A set sequence including forward folds, wide-legged poses and balance poses
3. **Main series.** In the primary series, the seated and floor poses follow, before the finishing sequence

After the main sequence comes the **finishing sequence**, which includes shoulderstand, headstand-related poses (depending on the teacher), and ends with rest in Savasana.

The full primary series (called Yoga Chikitsa, often described as "yoga therapy") takes between 75 and 90 minutes. Beginners start with a short version, often only the Sun Salutations and a few standing poses.

## The primary series, in plain language

The primary series is the first of the series in the Ashtanga system. Its aim is to build strength, flexibility and detoxifying heat in the body, though the claims about "detoxification" are traditional language, not established science. In outline, the sequence includes:

- **Sun Salutations A and B:** warm-up flows
- **Standing poses:** such as forward folds, triangle, side angle and wide-legged poses
- **Seated poses:** forward folds, twists and hip-openers, including poses such as Janu Sirsasana (head-to-knee pose) and Marichyasana (twists)
- **Backbends:** typically in the form of Bridge or Wheel in the later part
- **Finishing poses:** shoulderstand, plow and relaxation

Experienced students link the poses with vinyasa transitions, so the practice is nearly continuous.

## How a beginner can start

You do not need to do the whole primary series. A sensible starting plan is:

**Weeks 1 to 4: learn the basics**

- Practice Sun Salutation A, three to five rounds
- Learn the basic standing poses, holding each for five breaths
- Finish with a few minutes of Savasana

**Weeks 5 to 8: build up**

- Add Sun Salutation B, three rounds
- Add a handful of seated poses, such as a seated forward fold and a gentle twist
- Practice three to four times a week

**Beyond:** continue adding poses gradually. Work with a teacher who can tell you when it is appropriate to move forward. Many Ashtanga teachers encourage students to modify poses and to practice a shortened version for years.

A beginner-friendly option is to attend a led "introduction to Ashtanga" class, or a Mysore-style program with a qualified teacher.

## Sun Salutation A, step by step

Sun Salutation A is the foundation of the practice.

1. **Stand in Mountain pose** with feet together, hands at your sides
2. **Inhale, raise your arms overhead,** gazing up at your thumbs
3. **Exhale, fold forward,** bending your knees as needed
4. **Inhale, lift halfway,** with a long spine and hands on shins
5. **Exhale, step or jump back** to plank, then lower to low plank (chaturanga) with your elbows hugging your ribs
6. **Inhale, move into upward-facing dog** (or Cobra if you are a beginner)
7. **Exhale, move into downward-facing dog,** and stay for five breaths
8. **Inhale, step or look forward**, and step your feet toward your hands
9. **Exhale, fold,** then inhale to lift halfway
10. **Exhale, fold,** and inhale to rise and reach up
11. **Exhale, return to Mountain pose**

Beginners should step, not jump, and keep their knees down in chaturanga if they need to.

## Benefits and what research suggests

Because Ashtanga is a physically vigorous practice, it can build muscular endurance, balance and mobility, similar to other moderately intense exercise. Research on yoga in general, according to the U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), suggests possible benefits for stress management, mental and emotional health, sleep and balance, but the study quality varies and the results depend on the type of yoga. There is little research specific to Ashtanga, so claims that it cures conditions or "cleanses" the body should be treated with skepticism.

Many practitioners say the structure itself is part of the benefit: a regular routine, clear steps and focus on breath can make it easier to build a habit.

## A sample weekly plan for beginners

If you are not sure how to organize your first few weeks, this simple plan balances practice and rest. Adjust it to fit your life and energy.

- **Monday:** Sun Salutation A (five rounds) and standing poses, about 30 minutes
- **Tuesday:** rest, or a gentle walk
- **Wednesday:** Sun Salutations A and B (three rounds each) and a few seated poses, about 35 minutes
- **Thursday:** rest
- **Friday:** Sun Salutation A, standing poses and a short finishing sequence ending in Savasana, about 40 minutes
- **Saturday:** rest or restorative yoga
- **Sunday:** optional short practice, 15 to 20 minutes, or a day off

After four to six weeks, if you feel strong and free of pain, you can extend the sessions. Keep a short note after each practice about how your body felt. It helps you notice patterns, such as tight wrists or tired hamstrings, before they become injuries.

## Setting up your space and equipment

Ashtanga needs very little equipment, but some choices make practice more comfortable and safer:

- **A non-slip mat,** firm enough to protect your wrists and knees. Very thick, soft mats can make balance harder
- **A towel,** because the practice builds heat and sweat can make hands slippery
- **A strap and two blocks,** to make seated poses and forward folds more accessible
- **A quiet, ventilated room,** since the continuous movement warms the body
- **Comfortable, close-fitting clothing,** so that fabric does not get in the way in forward folds and inversions

Early morning is traditional, but any time on an empty stomach works. Many people wait two to three hours after a meal.

## Safety: this style needs extra care

Ashtanga is physically demanding, and injuries can occur, particularly in the shoulders, wrists, knees, hamstrings and lower back. The NCCIH notes that yoga is generally safe for healthy people when practiced appropriately, but that injuries can happen, and it advises practicing with a qualified instructor to reduce the risk.

Follow these principles:

- **Warm up properly,** and never skip the Sun Salutations
- **Modify generously.** Bend your knees, use props and skip poses that feel unsafe
- **Progress slowly.** Do not attempt advanced poses before you have the foundation
- **Avoid forcing seated poses,** especially those that twist or bend the knees, such as half lotus. Forcing the knee can cause injury, so use supports or skip the pose
- **Pay attention to wrists and shoulders** in repeated vinyasas
- **Rest when you need to.** Taking a day off, or a gentler practice, is not failure
- **Be careful with inversions and neck positions.** Avoid putting weight on the neck in shoulderstand, and consult a teacher

## Who should take extra care

Speak to a doctor or physiotherapist before starting Ashtanga if you:

- Are pregnant. The NCCIH notes that yoga may need modification during pregnancy, so check with your provider
- Have high blood pressure, glaucoma, sciatica or arthritis, which MedlinePlus lists among conditions where certain poses may need to be avoided or modified
- Have wrist, shoulder, knee, hip or back injuries
- Have neck problems, since several finishing poses involve weight near the neck
- Have osteoporosis or a history of fractures
- Have a heart condition or any condition that limits intense exercise

## Common mistakes

- **Doing too much too soon.** Trying the full series after a few classes is a common cause of injury
- **Jumping before you are ready.** Stepping is safer and just as valid
- **Ignoring pain.** Pain is a signal, not a challenge
- **Comparing yourself to advanced practitioners.** Many have practiced for years
- **Forgetting the breath.** If you are holding your breath, slow down
- **Practicing six days a week immediately.** Build up gradually. Traditional teachers rest on moon days and Saturdays, so rest is built into the system

## Rest days and the "moon day" tradition

Traditional Ashtanga teachers advise rest on full and new moon days, as well as on Saturdays. Whatever you think of the tradition, the principle of regular rest is sound for any intense exercise. If you are new, three or four practices per week, with rest days between, is a good place to start.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Ashtanga suitable for beginners?** It can be, if you start with a shortened practice, modify the poses and learn with a qualified teacher. A full series on day one is not recommended.

**How long does it take to learn the primary series?** It varies widely. Some people take months, others years. The point is not to finish the series but to practice with attention.

**Do I need to be flexible or strong?** No. The practice builds both. Start where you are.

**Can I practice Ashtanga at home?** Yes, but beginners benefit from instruction. If you practice at home, use clear resources and keep to a shortened sequence.

**Is Ashtanga a spiritual practice?** It can be, depending on the practitioner. The eight-limb framework has a philosophical and ethical background, but you can approach the physical practice without adopting any belief system.

## Final thoughts

Ashtanga offers structure, challenge and a clear path of progress. Approached with patience and care, it can be a rewarding way to build strength and focus. Start small, respect your limits and remember that the number of poses you can do is far less important than how you practice them.
